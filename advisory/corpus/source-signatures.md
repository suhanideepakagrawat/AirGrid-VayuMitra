## Nitrogen dioxide as a traffic indicator

NO2 in urban air is produced predominantly by high-temperature combustion in vehicle
engines. Elevated NO2 relative to the surrounding area is therefore a conventional
indicator of a traffic-dominated source mix, and is used in source apportionment as a
marker for the on-road transport sector.

## Sulphur dioxide as an industrial indicator

SO2 arises mainly from the combustion of sulphur-bearing fuels, including coal and heavy
fuel oil, in industrial boilers, thermal power generation, brick kilns and similar
stationary sources. Elevated SO2 relative to the surrounding area is a conventional
marker for the industrial sector rather than for transport.

## The PM10 to PM2.5 ratio as a dust indicator

Coarse particulate matter, the fraction between 2.5 and 10 micrometres, is dominated by
mechanically generated dust from road surfaces, construction and demolition, and
unpaved ground. Combustion sources produce predominantly fine particles. A high ratio of
PM10 to PM2.5 therefore indicates a mechanical or dust-dominated source rather than a
combustion-dominated one.

## Why the diurnal cycle matters

Concentrations of all these pollutants rise at night in the Indo-Gangetic plain because
the atmospheric boundary layer collapses after sunset, confining the same emissions to a
much smaller volume. A source attribution based on absolute concentration will therefore
misread the night as an emissions event. Comparison against the simultaneous
area-wide median, rather than against an absolute threshold, removes this effect.
